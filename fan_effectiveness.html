Hangar exhaust fan effectiveness

Five days of measurement, May 2026. Investigating whether a roof-mounted exhaust fan helps or hurts the column temperature in a single-bay airplane hangar.

Measurements: 2026-05-09 to 2026-05-14 · Location: KLMO (Longmont, CO, 40.16°N, -105.16°W, elev 5055 ft) · Reading cadence: 1 min

How to read this document. Findings are tagged for confidence: Fact = measured directly. Inference = supported by data, alternative explanations conceivable. Conjecture = working hypothesis pending verification.

Setup

A single-bay airplane hangar at a regional airport (5055 ft elevation, semi-arid high plains, clear May days). The hangar has a metal roof, concrete slab floor, ground-coupled to ~50 °F deep-ground temperature. A Raspberry Pi runs a custom controller that logs three temperature sources every minute to SQLite:

  • Floor probe — DS18B20 at ~4 ft height. 0.1 °F resolution. Reading exposed as temp_c.
  • Ceiling probe — the indoor thermistor of a ceiling-mounted Durastar mini-split HVAC unit at ~12 ft height, read over LAN via the unit's WiFi dongle. 0.5 °C quantization (= 0.9 °F bin steps). Reading exposed as ac_indoor_f.
  • Outdoor ambient — fetched every 15 min from Open-Meteo at the airport's lat/lon. Reading exposed as ambient_c.

The hangar has an exhaust fan mounted in the roof, intended to vent hot air upward. It's controlled by a 120 V relay tied to a Pi GPIO; the user can switch it via a web UI (?fan_state=0|1 or ?fan_mode=auto, where auto triggers when the floor exceeds ~80 °F). The HVAC unit's compressor is gated by Freeze Prevention mode and does not run when the column is above ~46 °F, so it stayed in idle (146 W) throughout all five test windows — verified by the energy-meter readings in every minute's row. The question this document tries to answer: does running the fan reduce the column temperature in summer-like conditions?

What we measured

Five paired-window measurements on consecutive (mostly) clear May days. Each window starts near solar noon (12:00–12:05 MDT, except Day 1 which started at 14:35) and runs for 3 or 5 hours. The fan was ON for Days 1–4 and OFF for Day 5 (the control). Outside of the fan state, the equipment was identical day-to-day. Contamination flags (HVAC compressor minutes, hangar-door events detected from stratification anomalies, energy delta vs idle baseline) were clean on every window. Open-Meteo hourly shortwave radiation was pulled retroactively to characterize each day's solar input.

Finding 1: Roof solar gain drives most of the column heating

Fact On Day 5 with the fan locked OFF for the full 5-hour window, the ceiling temperature still rose +9.0 °F (from 84.2 to 94.1 °F at peak) and the floor temperature rose +7.2 °F (from 79.9 to 87.1 °F at peak). Outdoor ambient over the same window rose ~4 °F.

Inference The column responds to roof radiative heating independent of the fan. With ~950 W/m² incident solar on a clear day, a metal roof reaches ~130–150 °F; even without the fan running, this drives the column up through conduction and natural convection. The fan is therefore not the primary heat source — but, as Finding 2 shows, it does add a measurable amount on top.

Finding 3: Stratification gap widens when the fan is on

Fact The vertical temperature gradient (ceiling minus floor) is wider on fan-ON days than on the fan-OFF day, throughout the test:

Stratification gap (ceiling – floor) over elapsed time since T0

Days 2–4 (fan ON, reds) maintain a ~7–10 °F vertical gradient. Day 5 (fan OFF, blue) stays ~5–7 °F.

Inference A correctly-installed exhaust fan should increase mixing in the column — pulling air upward through the roof — and therefore narrow the floor-ceiling gradient. The observation that the gap is wider with the fan on is the opposite signature: it's consistent with a fan that is injecting air downward from the roof level, depositing the hottest available air at the ceiling layer where buoyancy then keeps it stratified. This is the central clue motivating the "reversed install" hypothesis (next section).

Solar irradiance (Open-Meteo hourly) and the crossover window

All four hot days show a similar solar curve. The crossover (16:40–16:55 MDT band) aligns with the time when solar input on the flat roof drops below ~750 W/m² and the roof starts cooling.

Inference Day 5 with fan OFF showed the same crossover at 16:40 MDT (within 15 minutes of all other days). This rules out fan dynamics as the trigger. The cause is the sun's altitude dropping enough that incidence on the flat roof falls past a threshold: at 16:40 MDT the sun altitude is ~37° (cosine factor 0.60 vs the noon peak of 0.91), and atmospheric absorption increases at lower altitudes. The roof stops radiating heat into the column faster than the column loses it. Local terrain (Front Range foothills to the west, angular horizon ~4°) does not factor — the sun is still well clear of the mountains at this time.

Finding 5: Floor probe exceeds outdoor ambient when fan is running

Fact On Day 4 (fan ON), the floor probe peaked at 89.8 °F while peak ambient was 88.9 °F — the column floor exceeded outdoor air by ~1 °F. On Day 5 (fan OFF), the floor peaked at 87.1 °F while ambient peaked at 86.5 °F — also slightly above ambient, but by a smaller margin (~0.6 °F).

Floor temperature minus outdoor ambient, over elapsed time

Day 4 (fan ON) reaches ~+1 °F above ambient at peak. Day 5 (fan OFF) tracks ambient closely (rises to ~0–1 °F at peak from a -3 °F start).

Inference The floor of the hangar sits on a slab that is ground-coupled to ~50 °F deep-ground temperature. With no heat injection, the floor air should equilibrate below the outdoor air (the slab acts as a heat sink). Floor temperature exceeding outdoor ambient — even by 1 °F — implies a source of heat is being delivered into the column from somewhere other than ambient air. The leading candidate is hot air from the roof level being mixed downward.

Working hypothesis: the roof-mounted exhaust fan is installed in reverse

Conjecture If the rooftop exhaust fan is physically wired or installed to push outdoor air downward through the roof opening (instead of pulling hangar air upward), then on a sunny day it would be injecting boundary-layer air that has been preheated by the sun-baked roof surface and the rooftop fan housing itself. Single-phase capacitor-start fans can be wired to spin either direction; an installer error or a replaced motor with reversed leads would produce this exact behavior.

What we don't know

The following are open questions and acknowledged uncertainties. Critical input on any of these is welcome.

  • Is the fan actually reversed? The reversed-install hypothesis is the working theory because it fits the four signatures simultaneously (warming, wider stratification, floor-above-ambient, late-afternoon flip). It has not been physically verified. Alternative mechanisms (e.g., a healthy exhaust fan whose presence simply increases the rate of heat transfer between the column and the roof boundary layer through induced flow, or fan motor heat dump dominating in still air) could also account for some of the observed effect. The hangar smoke test should settle this within minutes.
  • What is producing the ~9 °F roof-only heating? Day 5 with fan OFF showed +9 °F ceiling and +7 °F floor rise during peak sun. The roof material, color, insulation rating, and the area of the fan opening are all unknown to the analyst. A correctly-installed fan in this same hangar would still leave the column heating substantially on a clear day. Whether the right operational fix is more insulation, a roof coating, a different fan, or something else is open.
  • Are there confounders we haven't controlled for? Days were run on consecutive calendar dates with mostly similar weather, but wind speed and direction were not logged. Humidity not logged. The HVAC unit's indoor fan may have been cycling at low speed even during compressor-idle FP mode, which would impose a baseline mixing component the analyst can't isolate.
  • How representative is May solar in this analysis? Five clear days in mid-May, latitude 40°. Summer solar will be ~10 % stronger, and the column will likely behave more extremely. Winter solar is ~50 % weaker and the column never gets above ambient by much. The conclusions here apply to "hot sunny days at this latitude in late spring"; they don't directly generalize to every condition.
  • Is the ceiling probe a reliable proxy for "the upper hangar volume"? The ceiling probe is the indoor thermistor of a wall-mounted (well, ceiling-mounted) HVAC unit. It samples one point in the column at one height. A wider-spread sensor array would clarify whether the column actually stratifies uniformly or whether the fan creates a localized hot zone near its outlet.
  • What is the fan's flow rate? CFM is unknown. We measured the temperature effect but not the air-mass flow. Without CFM we can't convert the observed warming to a heat-injection rate or compare against expected exhaust-fan performance.
  • Is the natural-cooling reference (τ ≈ 12.4 h) the right baseline? The thermal time constant used in earlier framings was derived from nighttime cooling episodes (22:00–06:00 MST), not from daytime. Daytime τ in similar conditions is poorly constrained by the existing data, partly because the column rarely satisfies the "hangar > ambient and cooling" precondition during summer-like daytime. Comparisons against the τ-natural prediction during peak sun are therefore weak.
  • Are five days enough? Each day's test was clean (no compressor activity, no door events, kWh delta within 0.01 of idle), but n=4 for fan-ON and n=1 for fan-OFF is small. The fan-OFF day's magnitude (Day 5 column reaches similar absolute temperatures as fan-ON days) is the single strongest constraint on the analysis; replicating it would meaningfully tighten the estimate.

Method notes

  • HVAC contamination check. The mini-split unit's reported power was 146 W flat throughout all five windows, with zero minutes above the 200 W compressor-on threshold. Cumulative kWh delta over each 5h window was 0.73–0.74 kWh, matching idle expected within ±0.01 kWh. The HVAC was not heating or cooling.
  • Door-event check. An algorithm comparing floor and ceiling delta over 5-min sliding windows detected no door-opening anomalies. No human entered the hangar during any test window.
  • Ceiling-probe quantization. The HVAC indoor thermistor reports in 0.5 °C steps (= 0.9 °F bin steps). Between bin transitions the trace appears flat; the floor probe (0.1 °F resolution) is the leading indicator of column motion.
  • Open-Meteo data. Hourly shortwave radiation and cloud cover were pulled from Open-Meteo's forecast/archive API at the airport coordinates for retroactive solar characterization.
  • Pi cron. The reading cron writes to a tmpfs RAM database (/run/heater.db) every minute, flushed to disk weekly; analysis queries union both DBs.
